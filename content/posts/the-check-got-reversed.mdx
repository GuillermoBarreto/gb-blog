Eleven days. That's how long Jensen Huang was gone from Nvidia.

I read the headline twice yesterday morning. The board that accepted his resignation on November 12 voted unanimously to bring him back on November 23. Same board. Same chair. Eleven days apart, they said the exact opposite thing.

Last week, on the earnings call, board chair Harvey C. Jones said the board "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." Yesterday Jones said Huang's return is "in the best interests of our stockholders, employees, partners and customers." The board said it was satisfied with Huang's candor and leadership ability, and that after input from senior US government officials it saw no national security risk from his return.

The market agreed fast. Nvidia shares jumped more than 30% on Monday, their third-best day on record. Over $900 billion in market value came back in a single day. That's a record.

I don't own the stock. I don't know where it goes from here. But I know what this looked like from the outside, and it did not look like a process. It looked like a group of people who made a decision in 24 hours and then spent 11 days realizing they couldn't live with it.

And the investigation isn't even done. The board ordered an investigation into whether Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort, and it's still going. He's scheduled to testify before Congress in early December. He came back before his own board finished asking its questions.

The same day, the Commerce Department blacklisted Megaspeed, the Singapore firm at the center of the chip-diversion allegations. So one half of the story got reversed and the other half got a blacklist. The check landed somewhere. Just not on the CEO.

## The other headline

Anthropic launched Claude Ship on Monday. It's a set of agents that takes a pull request through review, fixes, and an approval decision. Three pieces. Code Review, which was already generally available. PR Steward, in beta, which fixes failing CI checks, merge conflicts, and reviewer comments. And Claude Approvals, also in beta, which approves routine changes on its own if the team's policy file allows it.

The policy file is the part I keep thinking about. Your team writes it in plain language, stores it in the repo, and Claude approves whatever it says is routine. Riskier changes still go to a human. Anthropic suggests running it in observe-only mode first, which tells you plenty about how confident they are.

It's available on Claude Team and Enterprise plans, GitHub repos only. There's a new /ship command in Claude Code that pushes your change, opens the PR, and starts the whole loop.

Anthropic says its own Claude Code team more than doubled merged PRs from June to September while reverts fell by more than 40%. Those are their numbers, not mine, and nobody independent has checked them. I haven't tried Claude Ship. I don't know if it's good.

## The review is the job

This month has been one long story about the check arriving. The SEC opened a probe into Nvidia. Towns voted no on data centers. A developer in Australia paused its own gigawatt project after 432 written questions. The check kept showing up.

Yesterday it went two directions at once. In one building, a board accepted a CEO's resignation and brought him back before finishing its own investigation. In another, the company that sells the coding agent started selling the approver too.

Two weeks ago I wrote that writing code got fast and reading it didn't. A survey that week said 81% of teams had merged AI code they didn't fully understand. Now there's a product that will approve the PR for you, as long as your policy file says it's routine. Routine is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

Here's what I actually know how to do. I'm a WGU software engineering student. I'm in CodePath interview prep, and the whole drill is reading code and saying why it's right or wrong, out loud, while someone watches. I ship small commits every night and I read them before they go up. I spent my days in IT support, where the check is the job. The change window. The queue. The part where someone else looks at it before it goes live.

That's the skill that keeps showing up in every story this month. Not writing faster. Reading carefully. Saying no when something doesn't hold up, even when the person who wrote it is the smartest person in the room. Even when the person who wrote it is you.

A policy file can say a change is routine. A board can reverse itself in 11 days. Neither one is a review. The review is a person who actually read the thing.

I'll keep my commits small and read them myself. That's the part I'm not outsourcing yet.
