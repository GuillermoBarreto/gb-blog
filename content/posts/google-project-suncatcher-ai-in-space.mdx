Google announced today that it's launching a prototype satellite **next week** carrying its Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) into low Earth orbit. It's the first in-orbit test of [Project Suncatcher](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/google-plans-first-test-ai-chips-space-under-project-suncatcher-2026-09-24/), a research effort exploring whether space could one day host large-scale AI computing infrastructure.

Let me break down what's actually happening, because the headline is wilder than the reality — and the reality is more interesting.

## What's being tested

The mission rides on SpaceX's upcoming Transporter-18 rideshare launch, in partnership with satellite company Planet Labs. The goal is to answer a simple question: **can AI chips survive and work reliably in orbit?**

Space is brutal on electronics. Radiation causes bit flips — random errors in data. Launch forces shake everything apart. And cooling, which data centers spend enormous effort on, is a completely different problem in a vacuum where there's no air to move heat around. Google's approach combines heat pipes with radiators.

Google already tested the TPUs running AI workloads at a UC Davis facility, but says orbital testing is needed to see how the hardware performs in the real environment. Two more satellites are planned for 2027 to test the high-bandwidth laser links that future computing clusters would need to talk to each other.

Google is clear about one thing: this mission is about **gathering data and finding failure points**, not demonstrating a working orbital data center. Experts quoted in the reporting say commercial viability is still years away given launch costs, engineering constraints, and satellite production bottlenecks.

## Why this matters for software people

Here's my take: the most interesting part of this story isn't the satellite. It's the *reason* the satellite exists.

Companies like SpaceX and Starcloud are pursuing orbital data centers because of near-continuous sunlight — free, abundant power — and to sidestep electricity constraints on the ground. Read that again: **the world's biggest tech companies are running out of easy power on Earth, so they're looking at space.**

That's how real the AI infrastructure bottleneck has become. It's not about better models anymore. It's about megawatts, cooling, and grid interconnection. The constraint moved from software to physics. As someone learning this industry, I think that's the single most important shift to understand right now: the limiting factor in AI isn't intelligence, it's electricity.

## Meanwhile, on the ground

In the same news cycle, AI startup Island announced a [$400 million Series F](https://www.reuters.com/technology/ai-startup-island-valued-64-billion-latest-funding-round-2026-09-24/) valuing the Dallas-based company at $6.4 billion. Island builds enterprise security tools, and the funding is aimed at the next phase of growth as companies scale AI agents.

My read: every wave of computing creates a new security surface, and AI agents — software that acts on your behalf — are a big one. The money is following the problem. If you're a student like me wondering where the jobs will be in five years, "securing the things AI agents touch" looks like a solid bet.

## The bottom line

One company is launching chips into orbit because Earth is running out of cheap power. Another just raised $400 million because AI agents need guardrails. The AI story in 2026 isn't really about AI — it's about everything *around* AI: the power grid, the cooling systems, the security layers.

The people who understand the infrastructure underneath the models are going to be the ones in demand. That's a good thing to keep in mind while choosing what to learn next.

What would you bet on — the exotic stuff like orbital compute, or the boring fundamentals like power and security? I'm honestly not sure yet.
