I read this headline twice because I thought it was a joke. Google is launching AI chips *into space*. Like, orbit. Next week.

It's real. And the reason behind it taught me something I didn't expect about where this industry is heading.

## What's actually happening

Google is sending a prototype satellite up on SpaceX's Transporter-18 rideshare launch, carrying its Tensor Processing Units (TPUs) into low Earth orbit. It's the first in-orbit test of [Project Suncatcher](https://www.reuters.com/business/media-telecom/google-plans-first-test-ai-chips-space-under-project-suncatcher-2026-09-24/), built with satellite company Planet Labs.

The question they're trying to answer is beautifully simple: **can AI chips survive and work in orbit?**

Space is brutal on hardware. Radiation flips random bits in your data. The launch shakes everything violently. And cooling — the thing data centers obsess over — works completely differently in a vacuum where there's no air to move heat. Google's answer is heat pipes plus radiators.

They already ran the TPUs on AI workloads at a UC Davis facility, but Google says you can't simulate orbit — you have to go there. Two more satellites are planned for 2027 to test the laser links that future orbital clusters would need to talk to each other.

And to be clear: Google says this is about **finding failure points, not showing off**. The experts in the reporting say commercial orbital data centers are still years away — launch costs, engineering limits, satellite production bottlenecks. This is research, not a product.

## The part that stopped me

Here's what I keep coming back to: *why* does this project exist at all?

Companies are looking at orbit because of near-continuous sunlight — free power — and to dodge electricity constraints on the ground. Let that sink in. **The biggest tech companies on Earth are running out of easy power, so they're looking at space.**

I'm a student. I spend my nights thinking about algorithms and data structures. But this story reframed something for me: the AI bottleneck isn't smarter models anymore. It's megawatts. It's cooling. It's the power grid. The constraint moved from software to *physics*.

That's the single most useful thing I've learned this month. While I'm choosing what to study next, I'm paying attention to the infrastructure underneath the models — because that's where the demand is going.

## Meanwhile, back on Earth

Same news cycle: AI startup Island raised [$400 million at a $6.4 billion valuation](https://www.reuters.com/technology/ai-startup-island-valued-64-billion-latest-funding-round-2026-09-24/). They're a Dallas-based company building enterprise security tools, and the money is going toward securing AI agents as companies scale them up.

My read? Every computing wave creates a new security problem, and AI agents — software that acts on your behalf — are a big new surface. The money follows the problem. For someone like me wondering where the jobs will be in five years: "securing the things AI agents touch" sounds like a pretty good bet.

## What I'm taking from this

One company is launching chips into orbit because Earth is running out of cheap power. Another just raised $400 million because AI agents need guardrails. The AI story in 2026 isn't really about AI — it's about everything *around* it: the grid, the cooling, the security.

The people who understand the boring infrastructure underneath the exciting models? They're going to be in demand. Good to know while I'm still picking what to learn.

Honest question: would you bet on the exotic stuff like orbital compute, or the boring fundamentals like power and security? I'm still making up my mind.
