I checked the numbers this morning before I did anything else. Saturday, coffee, phone in my hand. And Friday was a bloodbath.

Nvidia fell 14% in one day. One day. About $491 billion in market value, gone. The company went from roughly $3.52 trillion to about $3.03 trillion between the open and the close.

The trigger was a Reuters report. The Wall Street Journal had said on Tuesday that the SEC opened a civil investigation into whether Nvidia's disclosures misled investors. On Friday, Reuters said the agency's enforcement staff are looking at potential accounting issues, citing two people briefed on the matter. Reuters couldn't say when the inquiry began or which practices are under review. The SEC declined to comment, and Nvidia didn't immediately respond.

An investigation is not a finding, and plenty of probes close without any enforcement action. But the market didn't wait for the details. The stock fell 14% on the news, its seventh straight daily drop and seventh straight weekly drop, the longest weekly losing streak since 2008.

And this is the part that scares me. On Tuesday, Nvidia reported its best quarter ever: $115.8 billion in revenue, up 103% from a year ago. The chips work. The demand is real. The numbers were great, and the stock still fell 14% three days later. Not because the product failed. Because of the paperwork around it.

The questions are specific. Nvidia breaks out revenue by where customers are headquartered, not where the chips end up. The Financial Times reported about $104 billion of 2025 revenue was booked through its Singapore unit. Interim CEO Colette Kress has said more than 99% of the data center revenue invoiced to Singapore came from US-based customers. That's the kind of sentence that sounds reassuring until the SEC is the one deciding whether it holds up.

Then there's the guarantees. On Tuesday's call, Kress said they totaled $118 billion, including $105 billion tied to a single data center campus. That's the company putting its own balance sheet behind someone else's buildout.

The pile keeps growing. California's attorney general served Nvidia a subpoena on Thursday, looking into whether its actions helped China get advanced chips. The Justice Department is reviewing the planned $12.9 billion purchase of Hugging Face. The 10-Q is delayed, the board has its own committee investigating, and there's still no permanent CEO.

Anthropic had its own rough Friday. The stock closed at $88.30, down about 9% on its second day. During the day it touched $83.05, five cents above the $83 IPO price. On Thursday it opened at $111.50 and closed at $97.17. In two days, almost the entire first-day pop is gone. The Nasdaq gave back all of its gains for the year.

I don't own either stock. I don't know if the SEC finds anything at Nvidia, and I don't know where Anthropic trades on Monday. But I know the shape of the week. Thursday was a record IPO and a record quarter. Friday was a receipt.

The same week, something smaller rhymed with all of it. PHP 8.6 shipped on Thursday. I haven't run it yet, but I read the notes. Partial function application, a clamp function, a proper Duration class, a polling API, default values for readonly properties. And safer session defaults. The language got stricter in the places where sloppy defaults used to bite people.

It landed the same day as a security sweep across every supported PHP branch. Four older versions got patched at once: 17 advisories, seven critical, including remote code execution bugs. Version 8.2 hits end of life on December 31, so if you're still on it, that's a real deadline.

I've been writing about versions of this all month. A prepayment is not a lease. A merge is not a review. A promise is not a result. Friday added one more: a great quarter is not a clean bill of health.

I'm a WGU software engineering student. I work IT support during the day and write code at night, and my evenings go to CodePath interview prep, reading code I didn't write and trying to say what's wrong with it. That's the job, really. Not writing the clever part. Reading the boring part and catching what it missed.

Nobody at my level is going to audit Nvidia's revenue recognition. But the skill is the same one, just smaller. Read the disclosure. Read the diff. Read the changelog. Run the test before you trust the green checkmark. The week the biggest company in AI lost half a trillion dollars in a day, it wasn't the GPUs that failed. It was the paperwork. And the paperwork is the part you can actually learn to read.
