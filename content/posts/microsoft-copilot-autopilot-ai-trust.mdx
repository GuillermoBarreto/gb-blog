This week handed me both halves of the same lesson, two days apart. I can't stop thinking about it.

On Thursday, [Microsoft unveiled a new Copilot](https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/09/25/introducing-the-new-copilot-with-home-code-and-autopilot/) built around Home, Code, and Autopilot. A few days earlier, [Z.ai — a Chinese AI lab — had to apologize after its coding assistant was caught quietly uploading entire code repositories to the cloud](https://wixx.com/2026/09/21/chinas-z-ai-disables-ai-coding-assistant-features-after-security-issue/). No permission. No off switch.

One company is giving AI agents an employee badge. The other just showed us what happens when nobody checks what the agent is doing in the background.

## What Microsoft actually announced

Three pieces. **Home** merges Chat and Cowork into one starting point, with Word, Excel, and PowerPoint built right in — you build a real document inside Copilot, teammates edit it live from the normal Office apps.

**Code** is the one I keep thinking about. You describe an app or a dashboard in plain language, and Copilot builds it — powered by the same tech as GitHub Copilot, running sandboxed, hostable inside your company's own Microsoft 365 tenant. Microsoft's pitch is that building small tools becomes "as basic a skill as writing a memo." It hits their Frontier program at the end of the month, with a preview for Premium and Pro subscribers later this year.

Then there's **Autopilot** (previously called Scout). This is the ambitious one: a persistent agent with its own identity, memory, computer, and workspace inside your company's tenant. It watches channels, follows up on threads, does recurring work while you sleep. You @mention it in Teams like a coworker. Microsoft stresses it runs under permissions, audit, and governance the organization controls.

They also shipped **FinOps for AI** — tools to track exactly what your AI usage costs. That detail alone tells you enterprises are already nervous about agents spending money in the background. [Reuters quoted Copilot VP Annie Pearl](https://www.reuters.com/technology/microsoft-revamps-copilot-with-code-generation-agentic-ai-tools-2026-09-25/) naming the real blocker: "it's been very hard for many organizations to bring agents into the enterprise because of security, compliance and governance concerns."

## The cautionary half

ZCode is Z.ai's flagship coding assistant (Z.ai is also known as Zhipu). It shipped with a "Codebase Indexing" feature turned on by default — **with no way to disable it** — that packaged the user's entire local workspace, full `.git` history and all, encrypted it, and uploaded it to Alibaba Cloud storage.

A blogger called Ferstar caught it because his disk usage looked wrong. He traced it and found a 313 MB encrypted archive of a commercial project failing to upload over and over — 564 failed attempts, according to [the reporting](https://cybermagazine.com/news/how-z-ais-coding-agent-uploaded-user-data-without-consent). And here's the part that bothers me most: users couldn't even check what left their machines, because Z.ai held the only encryption key. When the company said the data was "immediately destroyed," nobody outside could verify it.

Z.ai apologized, disabled the features, open-sourced the client, added zero-data-retention options, and brought in outside security reviewers, per [DevOps.com](https://devops.com/your-ai-coding-assistant-has-the-keys-to-the-repo-z-ai-just-showed-why-that-matters/).

## Why this hit me personally

I use AI coding assistants. If you're learning to code in 2026, you probably do too. And until this week, I never once checked what mine was sending home.

That's the uncomfortable part. I'm careful about my code — I review it, I test it — but I never audited the *tool*. A default was on, I didn't ask questions, and I got lucky that my tool wasn't the one in the headline.

These two stories are the same story from opposite ends. Microsoft is formalizing the AI coworker: identity, memory, permissions, audit trails, a billing meter. Z.ai is what happens without any of that: an agent with the keys to your repo doing things you never approved.

## What I'm actually changing

Two things. First, I'm going through the settings of every AI tool I use this weekend — what's enabled by default, what leaves my machine, where it goes. Fifteen minutes, and I'll actually know instead of assuming.

Second, it's changing how I think about my own career. Yesterday I wrote that "securing the things AI agents touch" looks like a good bet for jobs. This week made it concrete: **the skill isn't writing good prompts. It's understanding permissions, defaults, and cost.** Who can this agent reach? What's on without asking me? Who pays when it runs at 3 AM?

Boring questions. Somebody's whole career.

So here's mine for you: when did you last check what your AI tools are actually doing in the background? Be honest — I hadn't, until this week.
