Thanksgiving morning. I'm up before everyone else, coffee in my hand, scrolling before the day starts. And the first thing I see is a number so big it doesn't sound real.

A million. Data centers. In space.

Yesterday the FCC approved SpaceX's plan to put up to a million satellites in orbit and run them as data centers. The network is called Starmind. The satellites would do the AI computing up there, powered by the sun, and send the results back down to Earth. It's the first time the US has ever authorized anything like it.

I read that twice. Then I read the second story in my feed, and the two of them together are the whole month in miniature.

The approval comes with a schedule, and the schedule is the tell. First satellite by November 2033. Then 100,000 by 2035. Half a million by 2038. The full million by 2040. SpaceX says it will start launching in 2027, but the license gives it fourteen years to finish. A permission slip is not a launch.

They'd fly between 500 and 1,002 kilometers up, talking to each other by laser, with radio as backup. Nvidia is in on it too. The first Starmind satellites would use a modified version of its Vera Rubin NVL72 system.

Not everyone cheered. Amazon's satellite business, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society all objected, over debris, congestion, radio interference, and what a million bright objects do to the night sky. The FCC said the plan fits its debris rules but did not approve the plan for satellites above 600 kilometers. That part is still open. SpaceX already has about 11,000 Starlink satellites up there. Starmind would multiply that roughly fifty times.

Now the other story. While the FCC was approving data centers in space, banks in Asia were freezing the money for data centers on the ground.

Bloomberg reported this morning that lenders have paused billions in Asian data center loans tied to ByteDance, Alibaba, and Tencent. The biggest one in limbo is a $6.6 billion loan for Bain Capital's Bridge Data Centres, whose Malaysian sites serve ByteDance. A $480 million loan for a Singapore neocloud called Aolani, also tied to ByteDance, is under review. One bank walked away from a $372 million loan for Digital Halo, whose Malaysian facility serves Alibaba. That loan was already fully underwritten. A fund dropped out of a $300 million loan to GMI Cloud for a Thai site used by Tencent.

The trigger is the export rule from last week. Companies in Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, and Brazil now need a US license to buy advanced AI chips, and data centers already leasing a lot of compute to Chinese firms have 90 days to get a separate license to keep doing it. Citigroup, Standard Chartered, and Singapore's big banks are all reviewing their data center clients. One bank is trying to offload about a billion dollars of that risk.

Lending to data centers in the region went from almost nothing to more than $49 billion since 2023. That is a lot of money built on a rule that just changed.

## The part that got me

Put them next to each other. On the ground, you can't get a loan for a data center in Malaysia this week. In orbit, you can get permission for a million of them.

Yesterday I wrote about HPE saying it won't finance neoclouds. The lender drew a line. Today the line spread to Asia. A loan that was basically done is stuck in due diligence. A bank walked away from a deal that was already underwritten. That is how fast a rule turns into a freeze.

And the industry's answer is to leave the ground entirely. I get the logic. On Earth, a data center needs land, power, water, permits, and now a license and a lender who isn't scared. In space the sun is free and nobody's town council votes on it. A September poll found 65 percent of Americans oppose building AI data centers in their communities, with only 11 percent in favor.

I don't know if orbital data centers ever make sense. I really don't. A million satellites is a number for a press release until somebody launches the first one, and the first one isn't due for seven years. The debris question isn't settled. The astronomy question isn't settled. The approval is real, and it matters, but it is a piece of paper. The data center is not.

I know the pattern, because I've been writing about it all month. The build keeps outrunning the check. Then the check shows up. Sometimes it's a regulator. Sometimes it's a lender. Sometimes it's a town with 432 questions. The check is never the fun part. It's the boring layer. Credit. Permits. Debris rules. Licenses.

That's the job, though. I'm a WGU software engineering student. I push code every night, small commits, and I spend my days in IT support, where a change window is a real thing with a real rollback plan. My CodePath drills teach the same lesson: the review is the work. The code you write is the easy part. Getting it past the check, that's the job.

SpaceX just got past one check. The big one. A million satellites, approved. Now comes the part nobody writes headlines about. Build the first one. Launch it. Keep it from hitting anything. Do it 999,999 more times.

Anyway. Turkey's in a few hours. The satellites aren't. That's the difference between a permission slip and a data center.
