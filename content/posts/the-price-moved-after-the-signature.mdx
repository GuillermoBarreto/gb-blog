Monday morning. Coffee. I'm catching up on the weekend's news before work.

The Financial Times reported on Sunday that Dell, HPE, Lenovo, and Supermicro are asking customers to pay a lot more for Nvidia-powered AI servers than the price in the signed contract. Not a little more. Between 25% and 45% more in some cases, with the new number arriving when the equipment is ready to ship, months after the deal was struck.

A signed contract is supposed to be the end of the negotiation. You sign, you know what you owe, you plan around it. According to the FT, that is not how it works right now.

The driver is memory. JPMorgan research cited in the report puts the rise in memory costs at more than 400% since the start of 2024. GPU servers are up at least 20% to 30% in some cases. CPU-based server nodes, where memory is a bigger share of the cost, have more than tripled. Memory now makes up more than half of a traditional server's bill of materials, HPE said in its earnings call.

And HPE is not pretending this is temporary. Its CEO, Antonio Neri, told investors the company has changed its quoting terms so existing orders can be repriced if component costs rise between the quote and shipment. He expects elevated memory and storage prices to persist well into 2027. HPE and Supermicro declined to comment to the FT. Dell and Lenovo did not respond.

One person who talked to the FT put it in a line I can't shake: "The standard now is we'll sign a contract with an indicative price and the price could be arbitrarily high by the time they deliver it." Indicative. That's the word doing all the work. The price in the contract is a suggestion now.

There is already a lawsuit about it. XTX Markets, a British trading firm, sued Dell in an Irish court in June. The FT's account of the filings says the two sides agreed a fixed price of $549 million for 1,680 servers, and XTX paid another $5 million specifically to secure price certainty. Then Dell told them the order would need another $62 million to $70 million to cover component costs. Dell disputes XTX's characterization. I don't know who wins that case. But someone paid extra for price certainty and still ended up in court.

The firms getting hit include some of the biggest trading names, Jane Street, Optiver, and Hudson River Trading, all building out data center capacity for their research. Buyers with real money and real lawyers, paying anyway, because they need the machines.

## The part that got me

The same morning I read the FT story, Applied Digital announced a new 150-megawatt, 18-year lease with an unnamed investment-grade hyperscaler. About $5.4 billion in base-term revenue, or $12.7 billion if all the renewal options get exercised over 33 years. Delivery starts in the first half of 2028. The company's total contracted base-term lease revenue is now over $41 billion, about $100 billion with renewals.

So here is the shape of the morning. The price of the servers inside the building moves after you sign, and the leases for the buildings themselves keep getting signed, bigger and longer, $5.4 billion at a time.

That is the thread I have been writing about all month, and it keeps finding a new shape. The signature is not the price anymore. The price is whatever it is on the day the truck leaves.

I work IT support during the day. I have never bought a server, and I have never seen one of these contracts. I am a WGU software engineering student who writes code at night and does interview prep on CodePath. So I am not going to pretend I know how a $549 million server deal works.

But I do know what a moving quote feels like from the other side of the desk. Somebody asks for a number. You give them one. The parts cost more when you go to order them, and now you are the person explaining why the number moved. It is the worst conversation in support work, because the person across from you did nothing wrong. They budgeted for the number you gave them. And the number was never really the number.

That is what is happening at a scale I can barely picture. The buildout does not pause when the price moves. It just passes the cost along, vendor to builder to hyperscaler to whoever rents the compute, until it lands on a team that budgeted for one number and got another, and a person who has to explain it.

A few honest caveats. The 45% figure is what the FT's sources say, not something Dell or HPE confirmed, and the buyers did not confirm their contract terms. I don't know what the contracts actually allow, or how the XTX case ends. What I do know is that the vendors are not denying the direction. HPE changed its terms in public and told investors to expect high prices into 2027. That is not a rumor. That is a policy.

The durable skill here is the same one it always is. Read the terms. A signature means what the fine print says it means, and right now the fine print says the price can move. If you are the person who has to explain a budget that moved, the only honest move is to say so early, with the real number, before the truck shows up.
